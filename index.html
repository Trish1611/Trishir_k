How I judge a model

Four things I check before a drawing goes out

The tree has to survive a change

A model that breaks three features downstream when one dimension moves is a sketch with extra steps. Features should reference geometry that means something, not whatever face happened to be there.

Evidence — cycloidal disc profile driven from its design equations, built so eccentricity and roller pitch propagate correctly.

Someone has to be able to make it

Internal corners a cutter can't reach, tolerances nobody can hold, a pocket that needs a tool longer than its own diameter. Manufacturability is a modelling decision, not a review finding.

Evidence — a year applying DFM constraints at concept stage, then standing in front of the machinist who found the dimension that couldn't be made.

Tolerances are design, not a final pass

Which dimension carries the function, which datums the part locates from, where the stack-up lands. Decided while modelling, or the drawing is just dimensions with a title block.

Evidence — stack-up across every mating precision component of a reducer whose assembly depends on a single eccentricity tolerance.

Documentation is part of the model

If a colleague can't open it, understand the intent and re-release it without rebuilding, the work isn't finished. Drawings, BOMs and revision control are the model's interface.

Evidence — drawing packages maintained under SolidWorks PDM and carried through revisions to procurement.

Two-stage cycloidal speed reducer

DRDO R&DE (Engineers), Pune · 2022–2023 · B.E. dissertation, three-person team

The hardest model I have built. A cycloidal disc profile is not drawn — it is generated from linked parameters, and the whole assembly's function rests on one tolerance.

SolidWorksEquation-driven geometryTolerance stack-upGD&TMATLABANSYS
+ Read the detail

Why the modelling was the hard part

The cycloidal profile comes out of the rolling-circle diameter, the eccentricity and the roller pitch circle. These are not three independent numbers you can nudge — they are one linked set, and changing any of them to fit a packaging constraint forces the others to move with it. A model built without that structure looks right once and then quietly produces a non-cycloid the first time somebody edits a dimension.

What I did

  • Generated the disc profiles for both stage ratios in SolidWorks from the design equations, and sized the output-pin holes that convert the eccentric motion to concentric output.
  • Carried tolerance stack-up and manufacturability analysis across every mating precision component before drawings were released. Eccentricity is the dimension the assembly depends on: miss it and nothing aligns.
  • Compared a single-stage and a two-stage layout, and selected two stages because forcing the whole ratio into one drives disc diameter and contact stress up sharply.
  • Wrote the MATLAB routines computing contact-force distribution across the housing rollers as a function of input angle, as a calculation tool for the team.

What it did not settle

Nothing was built, so there is no measured backlash and no transmission-error data — and those are the two numbers that decide whether such a drive is usable. Manufacturing tolerance on the eccentricity was not carried through the analysis either. Knowing which questions a model leaves open is half of reading one.

My role: three-student dissertation team under Mr. Dipak Bhirud. CAD models and disc profile geometry, FE analysis, substantial contribution to ratio calculations, motion simulation and the report. Design and analysis only. Design inputs, envelope constraints and numerical results are withheld under the organisation's confidentiality requirements.

Process selection as a design decision

Each component got the process that could actually deliver what it needed — CNC where accuracy and stiffness mattered, laser cutting for flat brackets, 3D printing for anything being iterated that week, off-the-shelf where off-the-shelf was good enough. Having the machine in the next room changes how you model: you stop drawing features that are expensive to make.

Lightening, then verifying

The rocker brackets were pocketed to take mass out, then checked in ANSYS for deformation and stress under load before anything was cut. The result above shows roughly 0.026 mm peak deflection — enough margin to keep the geometry honest without carrying metal that wasn't working.

Fitment before commitment

  • Built locating and drilling jigs so the suspension hardpoints could be assembled repeatably, and checked fitment dimensionally before anything was welded or bonded.
  • Integrated with chassis, drivetrain, sensors and control electronics, running the wiring and cable routing alongside the electrical and software sub-teams.
  • Commissioned the car, measured dynamic performance across successive test sessions, diagnosed faults trackside and cut the replacement part the same day.
  • Maintained the parts list and coordinated supplier lead times against race deadlines.

Stair-climbing rover mechanism

MGM's Polytechnic, Aurangabad · 2019–2020 · four-person team, led

A six-wheel rocker-bogie platform built from components a student team can actually buy. I led it: mechanism design, procurement, assembly, and both write-ups — including the part that failed.

Mechanism designRapid prototypingProcurementAssemblyTeam lead
+ Read the detail

Concept selection

The design driver was keeping all wheels loaded on an uneven surface. A tracked platform was the whole project on its own and couldn't be sourced locally; a rigid chassis loses traction the moment one wheel lifts. We chose a rocker-bogie linkage because it solves the problem passively — one wheel pair climbs while the rest keep the body level, with no actuator, no controller and nothing to tune.

Built for iteration

PVC pipe and standard fittings, chosen deliberately: repeatable joints without machining, light enough that the drives weren't fighting the structure, and revising a link length meant cutting a new section rather than re-fabricating a part. We dry-fitted the whole linkage before bonding anything, so arm geometry could be adjusted against a real staircase rather than a drawing.

The part that failed

It climbed domestic stairs and crossed uneven ground with the body staying far more level than a rigid chassis would. On risers approaching the wheel diameter, the leading wheel pushed into the face of the step instead of climbing it and the vehicle stalled. That limit is stated in the published paper. A free-body analysis of the leading wheel at first contact would have predicted it before the build — which is the lesson I actually took from the project.

My role: four-student team with a faculty guide; I led it. Mechanism design and layout, sourcing and procuring every component, physical assembly, and writing both the report and the published paper. Electronics wiring and control setup were shared with a teammate.

Does the simulation deserve to be believed?

SKNCOE, SPPU, Pune · 2023 · five-person team

An experimental and CFD study of vortex generators — and, underneath it, an exercise in checking a computational model against measured reality across a full test matrix.

CFDTest rigInstrumentationMeasured vs predictedPublished
+ Read the detail

Method

  • Built the bench: centrifugal blower, DN 40 valve, an orifice plate made to ASME standard and calibrated against a vane anemometer, and an electrically heated test channel with an insulated outer surface.
  • Instrumented it — Pt100 RTDs for inlet and outlet bulk air temperature, Type-T thermocouples along the absorber plate, a U-tube manometer across the test section, all logged through a DAQ unit.
  • Ran CFD on the channel and winglet geometry, then used the rig to check the simulation rather than trusting it.

Result

Measured and simulated heat transfer coefficients followed the same trend within small error across a Reynolds range of roughly 3,100 to 16,100. Perforated concave rectangular winglets reduced pressure drop against the unperforated case, at a slight cost in heat transfer improvement — which is the trade-off the study set out to quantify.

My role: five-person team under Prof. Ganesh Gade. Co-author; contributed to the geometry and CFD work, rig build and instrumentation, and the paper.
